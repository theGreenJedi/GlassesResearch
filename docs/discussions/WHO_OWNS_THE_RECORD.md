# Who Owns the Record?

## Smart glasses, surveillance, privacy, and individual power

This is a conversation, not a conclusion.

Smart glasses may force society to revisit an old assumption: that surveillance is something institutions do to individuals.

Governments, employers, stores, streets, vehicles, doorbells, phones, and buildings already record enormous portions of ordinary life. Wearable cameras change the arrangement because the individual can carry a persistent point-of-view record too.

Does that make surveillance worse — or does it redistribute some of the power surveillance creates?

## The opening proposition

One argument is that personal recording can create accountability rather than merely threaten privacy.

Imagine an employee whose work is questioned. The employer may have cameras, access logs, ticket systems, badge records, and management reports. But what if the employee also has a contemporaneous, owner-controlled record of what they actually did?

The same question applies to interactions with institutions, disputes, accidents, safety incidents, harassment, encounters with authorities, and simple failures of human memory.

A personal record could provide evidence where previously only the institution possessed evidence.

That suggests a provocative possibility:

> Surveillance concentrates power when only the institution owns the record. Recording may redistribute power when the individual owns one too.

But that proposition may be wrong — or dangerously incomplete.

## The strongest objection

The person wearing the glasses is not the only person being recorded.

Bystanders may have legitimate reasons not to want a permanent record of an ordinary conversation, an embarrassing moment, a medical visit, an intimate interaction, their children, their location, or simply their existence at a particular place and time.

Smart glasses also differ from many existing cameras. They travel with a person. They point where the wearer looks. They can potentially identify faces, transcribe conversations, infer context, search memories, and allow AI systems to interpret events long after they occurred.

A world containing cameras is not necessarily the same thing as a world containing millions of searchable artificial memories.

## Discreteness creates a paradox

People generally want wearable computers to look like ordinary glasses. Large cameras, glowing displays, and conspicuous electronics make glasses less wearable.

But the better smart glasses become at disappearing into ordinary eyewear, the harder it becomes for another person to know whether they are being recorded.

The characteristic that may make smart glasses socially successful could therefore be the same characteristic that makes them socially unacceptable.

## Maybe ownership matters more than recording

There is another distinction worth testing.

Suppose two pairs of glasses capture exactly the same information.

With one pair, video, audio, transcripts, identity data, and AI-derived memories are uploaded to a manufacturer's servers and retained under terms the wearer does not meaningfully control.

With the other, the information remains encrypted on hardware controlled by the wearer, is never uploaded without explicit action, and can be permanently deleted by the wearer.

Are those ethically equivalent?

Legally they may sometimes be treated similarly. Technically and socially, perhaps they should not be.

This is why GlassesResearch tracks **Owner Control** and **Cloud Independence** separately from raw hardware capability.

## Questions we do not know the answers to

- Should people assume they may be recorded in public?
- Does an individual have a right to preserve their own experience?
- When does that right conflict with another person's reasonable expectation of privacy?
- Should recording indicators be mandatory?
- Should laws distinguish recording from AI analysis of a recording?
- Should facial recognition receive different treatment from ordinary video?
- Does local-only storage materially change the privacy question?
- Should an employer be permitted to prohibit an employee's personal record while maintaining its own surveillance system?
- Should individuals have the same evidentiary capabilities as institutions?
- Can smart glasses be both discreet and socially trustworthy?
- Who should be able to subpoena or otherwise compel access to a wearer's private record?
- If an AI creates searchable memories from a person's life, who owns those memories?

## Our position

We don't have one yet.

Our opening hypothesis is that wearable recording could sometimes democratize accountability by giving individuals access to evidence and memory capabilities historically concentrated in institutions.

But widespread wearable recording could also eliminate forms of practical privacy that people reasonably value.

Both things may be true simultaneously.

So don't accept our premise.

**Convince us.**

Bring evidence. Bring research. Bring law. Bring technical solutions. Bring counterexamples. Bring personal experience. Make the strongest argument you can — especially if you think the opening proposition is wrong.

If a challenge changes our understanding, we want the record of that change preserved rather than silently rewriting the question until it appears we knew the answer all along.

# You bought the glasses. Can you repair them?

*The lawsuit is new. The question is older: what does ownership mean when eyewear becomes computing hardware?*

**Published:** September 19, 2026  
**Status:** Verified  
**Evidence status:** STEADY  
**Type:** GlassesResearch editorial / right to repair  
**Evidence posture:** lawsuit allegations are attributed and unadjudicated; statutory and plaintiff-history context is independently sourced

Smart glasses are becoming computers we wear on our faces. That makes an old question newly important: **when they break, who owns the repair?**

Denver repair company Acme Revival has sued Luxottica Group, Luxottica of America, Meta Platforms and Meta Platforms Technologies, alleging that they failed to provide repair resources for Ray-Ban Meta smart glasses required by Colorado's Consumer Repair Bill of Rights Act.

Acme says it received at least 400 requests in 2026 to diagnose, maintain or repair Ray-Ban Meta products. It alleges that some repairs could not be accepted or completed because parts, documentation, software, firmware, tools and other resources were unavailable. The case, *Acme Revival, Inc. v. Luxottica Group S.p.A., et al.*, No. 2026CV33235, was filed September 5 in Denver District Court.

Those are allegations. They have not been adjudicated, and the defendants will have an opportunity to respond.

There is also relevant context concerning the plaintiff. In 2025, Colorado Attorney General Phil Weiser sued Acme Revival and its owner after a state investigation alleged deceptive repair practices, undisclosed or poorly disclosed fees, retention of customer devices and other consumer-protection violations. Those allegations are separate from the current right-to-repair case. They neither establish nor disprove Acme's claims against Meta and Luxottica, but they matter when evaluating the provenance of claims made by the company.

## Repairing a computer you wear

Right to repair has traditionally been discussed around phones, laptops, tractors, appliances and automobiles. Smart glasses complicate the idea.

A modern pair can contain cameras, microphones, speakers, batteries, radios, processors, sensors, touch controls and charging electronics. Some depend heavily on firmware, companion applications or manufacturer-controlled services. Replacing a physical component may therefore be insufficient if calibration software, firmware, pairing procedures, diagnostics or authorization remain controlled by the manufacturer.

**A device can be physically repairable while remaining functionally unrepairable by its owner.**

Meaningful repairability can therefore include parts, documentation, diagnostics, firmware, calibration procedures and the software needed to commission replacement components.

## Ownership after the warranty expires

Smart glasses are simultaneously electronics and eyewear. People may keep conventional frames for years; consumer electronics often operate on much shorter product cycles.

What happens when the frame remains usable but the battery degrades? What happens when a microphone, charging contact, camera or touch sensor fails? What happens when the hardware still works but the software needed to configure it disappears?

Whole-device replacement may be convenient during a warranty period. **It is not the same thing as repair.**

## Open is not the same as repairable

A platform can expose an excellent developer API while remaining difficult to repair. Hardware can be physically accessible while replacement components require proprietary software to initialize. A device can support alternative applications while remaining dependent on manufacturer infrastructure for servicing.

GlassesResearch already evaluates **Openness, Owner Control, Cloud Independence and Hackability**. Repairability intersects with all four, but it is not identical to any of them.

The practical question is simpler:

**If something breaks, can the owner realistically get it fixed?**

Behind that is the larger question:

**Does ownership survive failure?**

## Engineering constraints are not commercial restrictions

Smart glasses are compact. Components may be bonded or structurally integrated. Waterproofing, optical alignment, battery safety and prescription eyewear can create legitimate repair constraints.

Right to repair does not eliminate those constraints.

But engineering constraints and commercial restrictions are not the same thing.

Colorado's law is relevant precisely because covered repair resources can extend beyond physical parts to documentation, embedded software, firmware and tools. The Acme case may eventually clarify whether and how those requirements apply to Ray-Ban Meta glasses and which responsibilities belong to Meta, Luxottica or both.

Until a court decides those questions, the allegations remain allegations.

## What GlassesResearch will watch

For every pair of smart glasses we examine, repairability can be investigated empirically: Can the battery or temples be serviced? Are replacement parts and manuals available? Can an independent technician obtain diagnostics? Does component replacement require manufacturer-controlled software? Can firmware be restored? Can the device remain useful if manufacturer support ends?

Those questions are not peripheral to smart glasses.

They are questions about ownership.

**A pair of glasses shouldn't become disposable simply because someone put a computer inside the frame.**

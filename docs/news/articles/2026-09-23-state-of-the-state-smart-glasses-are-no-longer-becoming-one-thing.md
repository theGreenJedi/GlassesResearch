# State of the State: Smart glasses are no longer becoming one thing

**Published:** September 23, 2026  

For years, smart glasses were discussed as though the category were moving toward a single destination: ordinary-looking eyewear with a display, cameras, audio, an assistant, and enough compute to replace pieces of the phone.

The evidence now points somewhere more interesting.

**Smart glasses are separating into different kinds of tools.**

That separation is visible not only in hardware, but in what manufacturers think the glasses are *for*, who controls them, what data they collect, and how much authority the owner receives after purchase.

## One face, several categories

Some glasses are becoming cameras and AI companions. Some are displays. Some are hearing devices. Some are industrial tools. Some are deliberately camera-free. Some are becoming unusually open development platforms.

Those products can look remarkably similar on a table while representing very different relationships between the wearer, the manufacturer, and the surrounding world.

That is why GlassesResearch increasingly treats "smart glasses" as a physical form factor rather than a single product category.

## Specialization is becoming a feature

Nuance Audio is a useful example. It does not try to be a general-purpose visual computer. Its purpose is hearing assistance integrated into ordinary eyewear. The current Nuance Audio Plus generation pushes farther in that specialized direction with stronger amplification, improved speech clarity in noise, more battery capacity, physical controls, and phone-call support on compatible iPhones.

That is a very different proposition from a camera-first AI product or a heads-up display. It is not incomplete because it lacks those things. Its specialization *is the product*.

The same pattern appears in enterprise deployments, sports HUDs, tethered display glasses, accessibility products, and camera-free notification glasses.

## Openness is separating too

A second split concerns who gets to decide what the hardware can do.

Brilliant Labs' Halo firmware is a particularly concrete example. The published firmware is built around Zephyr on the Alif Balletto platform, exposes a Lua runtime, documents its BLE protocol, and supports owner-built firmware delivered through the device's OTA path. MCUboot test-boot and rollback behavior provide a recovery mechanism for experimentation.

That is not merely an SDK layered over a sealed appliance. It represents a materially different ownership model.

Mentra is pursuing openness from another direction. Mentra describes MentraOS as an open-source smart-glasses operating system and now positions its own Mentra Live hardware and applications primarily around frontline work. The important signal is larger than either company: interoperability and owner/developer access are becoming competitive dimensions rather than hobbyist afterthoughts.

Open does not automatically mean private, repairable, cloud-independent, or unrestricted. GlassesResearch will continue measuring those separately.

## The camera question is not going away

At the same time, camera-equipped glasses are encountering a social and institutional constraint that cannot be solved by making the electronics smaller.

A camera can be technically useful and socially unwelcome at the same time.

Recording indicators, workplace rules, transit policies, court restrictions, private-property rules, and simple bystander discomfort all shape whether a pair of glasses can actually be worn where its owner wants to use it. The more visually ordinary camera glasses become, the more important visible consent and predictable recording behavior become.

This creates another branch in the market: products that deliberately omit cameras are not necessarily technologically behind. For some users and environments, camera absence is a capability because it expands where the device can be worn without creating the same recording concern.

## Hardware lineage matters more as the outside converges

There is a paradox here. Smart glasses are becoming more visually alike while becoming less functionally alike.

Frames converge toward familiar eyewear shapes. Underneath them, however, products can differ radically in optical engines, chipsets, companion architecture, firmware, OEM/ODM ancestry, cloud dependency, sensors, and owner access.

That makes lineage research more important, not less.

Two products with different logos can share meaningful hardware ancestry. Two nearly identical frames can contain substantially different electronics. A model name can persist while the platform underneath it changes. Conversely, one hardware platform can surface under several brands.

GlassesResearch therefore treats lineage, aliases, rebrands, regulatory identities, and OEM/ODM relationships as evidence questions rather than cosmetic trivia.

## There may never be one winner

The category may not resolve into one pair of glasses that does everything.

That would make smart glasses less like smartphones and more like the broader eyewear market they are entering. People already choose glasses for different jobs: prescription correction, sun protection, safety, sport, fashion, reading, and accessibility. Adding electronics does not erase those distinctions.

It may multiply them.

The useful question is no longer simply **"Which smart glasses are best?"**

It is:

**What kind of glasses are these, what are they trying to do, and what does the owner actually control?**

That is the market GlassesResearch is built to map.

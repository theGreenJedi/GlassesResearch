# Who Owns the Record?

## Smart glasses, surveillance, privacy, and individual power

This is a conversation, not a conclusion.

Smart glasses are moving cameras, microphones, AI systems, and increasingly persistent memory into something people can wear all day. That creates an obvious privacy problem. It may also create something less frequently discussed: a way for ordinary people to possess evidence about their own lives.

Our starting hypothesis is deliberately contestable:

> **Ubiquitous personal recording may sometimes redistribute the power of surveillance rather than simply increase it.**

We may be wrong. Convince us.

## The case for the hypothesis

Modern life is already extensively recorded. Workplaces, stores, streets, vehicles, homes, police, phones, doorbells, and other systems routinely create records of events. Historically, however, much of that record has belonged to institutions rather than the individual being observed.

Wearable computing can change that asymmetry.

Imagine an employee who keeps a contemporaneous record of completed work and welcomes an audit because the evidence shows what actually happened. Extend that idea to owner-controlled smart glasses. A wearer might be able to document a safety hazard, reconstruct an accident, challenge an inaccurate account, preserve evidence of an interaction, or simply remember what they themselves experienced.

Under that view, the important question may not be only:

**Are the glasses recording?**

It may also be:

**Who owns the record?**

A record controlled exclusively by an employer, government, platform, or glasses manufacturer can concentrate power. A record controlled by the individual who created it may sometimes distribute power in the opposite direction.

That does not make recording harmless.

## The strongest objection

The wearer is not the only person in the wearer's field of view.

Camera glasses can capture people who did not choose the device, may not recognize it as a camera, and may have no practical way to refuse recording. France's data-protection authority, CNIL, warned in June 2026 that smart glasses can make capture of nearby people's images and voices nearly invisible and could normalize ubiquitous mobile surveillance. Its survey found 67% of respondents considered smart glasses a privacy risk.

Recent research also suggests a persistent wearer-bystander gap. A 2026 study involving 525 survey participants found that bystanders generally wanted stronger transparency and protections than wearers were willing to provide, with disagreement increasing in sensitive contexts.

And context plainly matters. A public sidewalk, workplace, courtroom, medical office, bathroom, private home, protest, and intimate conversation cannot reasonably be treated as equivalent environments.

## Recording is not the whole question anymore

The next generation of this debate is about more than video files.

Smart glasses can potentially combine capture with:

- speech transcription;
- object and scene recognition;
- facial or identity inference;
- searchable personal memory;
- location and time metadata;
- cloud processing;
- local AI processing;
- automated summaries and judgments;
- long-term retrieval.

A human forgetting an encounter is different from a computer retaining it indefinitely. A camera recording an image is different from an AI continuously interpreting everyone in view.

This suggests several separate questions that are often collapsed into the word *privacy*.

## Questions we want challenged

1. **Does personal recording democratize accountability, or merely democratize surveillance?**
2. **Should a person have a right to preserve a record of their own experience when that experience necessarily includes other people?**
3. **When should a bystander's desire not to be recorded override the wearer's desire to maintain a record?**
4. **Does local, owner-controlled storage materially change the ethical equation compared with vendor-cloud storage?**
5. **Should recording, AI analysis, biometric identification, and long-term memory be treated as separate capabilities under law and social norms?**
6. **Should smart glasses visibly identify themselves as recording devices, even if doing so makes them less discreet and less wearable?**
7. **Can bystanders communicate machine-readable privacy preferences to nearby wearables?**
8. **Should sensitive locations be able to trigger a hardware-verifiable camera or microphone disable mode?**
9. **Who should be able to subpoena, search, purchase, inherit, or otherwise obtain a wearer's personal visual memory?**
10. **If institutional surveillance is already widespread, does personal sousveillance restore balance—or make the underlying problem worse?**

## Possible technical middle ground

The choice does not necessarily have to be "record everything" versus "ban wearable cameras."

Researchers are already exploring privacy mechanisms such as visible recording indicators, context-adaptive protections, bystander-controlled privacy signaling, automatic redaction, and consent-based restoration of captured people. These approaches have trade-offs of their own: visibility versus disruption, wearer agency versus bystander control, and accountability versus exposure.

Owner control also matters. A device that processes locally, encrypts its history, exposes clear deletion controls, and does not silently upload observations creates a different risk model from glasses whose useful memory exists primarily on a manufacturer's servers.

## Our position today

GlassesResearch does **not** have a settled position on ubiquitous personal recording.

We think the possibility that personal wearables could redistribute some surveillance power toward individuals deserves serious consideration. We also think the strongest objections to that proposition deserve to be preserved rather than softened or hidden.

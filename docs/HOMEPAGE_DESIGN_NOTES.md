# Homepage design principle

The public website and the repository serve different audiences.

The repository may retain a rigorous institutional structure. The homepage should not mirror that structure. It should help a visitor quickly discover useful research.

The governing rule is:

> **Research first. Institution second.**

The homepage therefore prioritizes models, practical guides, current research, open development, historical preservation, and contribution paths. The founding charter, evidence standard, repository laws, preservation program, and institutional collection map remain available under **About & Governance**, where they support the work without competing with it.

## Publication-maturity rules

The homepage should behave like the front page of a specialist publication while remaining the front door to a research system. That means the research institute stays underneath the interface: provenance, uncertainty, Report Cards, corrections, lineages, preservation, and owner-control evidence must not be flattened into magazine-style certainty.

Reader order should normally be:

1. what GlassesResearch is and why its evidence posture matters;
2. one strong current editorial or research lead;
3. compact verified/developing newsroom summaries;
4. the Finder and other task-first entrances;
5. events and deeper ecosystem exploration.

Use verbs and reader jobs rather than internal collection names wherever the first click permits it: **Find glasses**, **Research a model**, **Follow what is changing**, **Compare**, **Check prescription support**, **Explore owner control**. Internal structures can appear after the reader has chosen the job.

Visual material should come from the research whenever practical: bench photographs, measured dimensions, frame profiles, screenshots, evidence diagrams, protocol maps, component relationships, and technology family trees. Generic decorative stock imagery should not become the site's visual language.

Freshness is part of the evidence state. Surface meaningful publication, verification, catalog, price-observation, and source-discovery dates where they help the reader understand what is current. Never create a fresh-looking timestamp merely because a page rebuilt.

Future homepage changes should favor:

- human-readable destinations rather than filenames;
- useful information rather than explanations of structure;
- a small number of clear paths rather than exhaustive navigation;
- visible recent work so the site feels active;
- differentiated visual treatment for editorial, verified, developing, Finder, and research surfaces;
- preservation of all underlying documentation without elevating every document to the front page.

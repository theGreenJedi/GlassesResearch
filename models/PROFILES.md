# Smart-Glasses Model Profiles

A model entry is not complete merely because it appears in a catalog. Each GlassesResearch profile should explain, in ordinary language, **what the glasses really are, what is interesting about them, where they are strong, and what tradeoffs matter**. The structured Report Card remains useful underneath; this page is the human-readable layer.

Profiles are published only when the available evidence supports something more useful than generic product description. Missing profiles are research work to be done, not invitations to manufacture filler.

## GLS-0001 — Ray-Ban Stories

Ray-Ban Stories is best understood as the proof that camera-and-audio electronics could disappear into recognizable everyday eyewear, not as a modern AI-glasses platform. Meta and EssilorLuxottica squeezed dual 5 MP cameras, open-ear speakers, three microphones, touch controls, a battery and a Snapdragon processor into familiar Ray-Ban frames while retaining prescription and conventional lens options. Its intelligence layer was comparatively modest and its media workflow depended heavily on the Facebook View phone app, but the product established a pattern the category still follows: **wearability first, phone-assisted computing second, no HUD at all**. Historically, its most important contribution may be that it made smart glasses look less like a headset and more like something a person might actually wear all day.

Source: [Meta — Introducing Ray-Ban Stories](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/09/introducing-ray-ban-stories-smart-glasses/)

## GLS-0002 — Ray-Ban Meta (Gen 1)

The 2023 Ray-Ban Meta generation turned the Stories experiment into a much more capable everyday-computing product. Meta moved to a single 12 MP ultrawide camera, improved the speakers and microphone array, enabled livestreaming, and made Meta AI a native voice experience; later software added Meta AI with Vision, allowing the system to answer questions about what the wearer was looking at. That makes Gen 1 much more consequential than a simple camera upgrade: it is where the Ray-Ban line crossed from **capture glasses into perception-and-assistant glasses**. Its major tradeoff is architectural rather than cosmetic—the hardware is extremely wearable, but much of the intelligence, account model and evolving feature set remains tied to Meta's software and cloud ecosystem.

Sources: [Meta — Introducing the New Ray-Ban Meta Smart Glasses](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/09/new-ray-ban-meta-smart-glasses/), [Meta — Meta AI with Vision update](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/04/new-ray-ban-meta-smart-glasses-styles-and-meta-ai-updates/)

## GLS-0003 — Ray-Ban Meta (Gen 2)

Ray-Ban Meta Gen 2 is an evolutionary product in the best sense: Meta kept the form factor people were already willing to wear and attacked two practical limitations—battery life and capture quality. The 2025 generation reaches up to eight hours of typical use and records 3K video, while retaining the camera, open-ear audio and multimodal Meta AI experience that defined the previous generation. That combination makes it one of the strongest benchmarks for **mainstream wearability plus useful visual AI**, but not for owner control: its usefulness grows through Meta's app, models and service integrations rather than through an open SDK or replaceable intelligence layer. It is therefore an excellent example of how a product can score very highly as consumer hardware while remaining much less compelling as an owner-controlled computing platform.

Source: [Meta — Ray-Ban Meta Gen 2](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/09/ray-ban-meta-gen-2-better-battery-life-video-capture/)

## GLS-0004 — Meta Ray-Ban Display

Meta Ray-Ban Display is where Meta's consumer glasses stop being screen-free and begin edging toward true visual computing. The glasses add a full-color, high-resolution display and ship with the Meta Neural Band, an EMG wristband that turns subtle muscle signals into interface commands. That matters more than simply adding a HUD: it creates a new interaction model in which information can appear privately in the wearer's view without requiring constant voice commands or reaching for a phone. The result is more capable than camera/audio Ray-Bans but also more system-dependent—the value comes from the tightly integrated glasses, neural input device, Meta AI software and service stack working together. It is a major capability step, but also a useful test of whether **richer interfaces inevitably mean deeper ecosystem dependence**.

Source: [Meta — Meta Ray-Ban Display](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/09/meta-ray-ban-display-ai-glasses-emg-wristband/)

## GLS-0005 — Ray-Ban Meta Blayzer Optics (Gen 2)

Blayzer Optics is significant because Meta stopped treating prescription support as an accessory to sunglasses and designed a Gen 2 frame specifically around people who rely on corrective eyewear all day. The rectangular Blayzer uses overextension hinges, interchangeable nose pads and optician-adjustable temple tips, and Meta says the optical line supports nearly all prescriptions. Underneath, it remains part of the Ray-Ban Meta Gen 2 AI platform, so the important change is not a new intelligence architecture but **better physical ownership of the form factor**: fit, lens compatibility and day-long comfort become primary engineering requirements rather than secondary options. For anyone who actually needs glasses to see, that can matter more than another incremental camera feature.

Source: [Meta — First AI Glasses Built for Prescriptions](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/03/meta-ai-glasses-built-for-prescriptions/)

## GLS-0006 — Ray-Ban Meta Scriber Optics (Gen 2)

Scriber Optics shares the prescription-first engineering of Blayzer but packages it in a rounder frame, giving the optical Gen 2 line a second everyday style rather than forcing prescription users into a single industrial-looking solution. Its overextension hinges, replaceable nose pads and adjustable temple tips reinforce the same idea: smart glasses meant to replace ordinary eyewear must be fitted like ordinary eyewear. Technically it inherits the strengths and constraints of the Gen 2 Meta platform—strong capture, audio and visual AI, paired with substantial dependence on Meta's application and cloud services. The interesting part is therefore not raw capability but **normalization**: the computer is being designed around the optician's chair as much as around the electronics bench.

Source: [Meta — First AI Glasses Built for Prescriptions](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/03/meta-ai-glasses-built-for-prescriptions/)

## GLS-0007 — Oakley Meta HSTN

Oakley Meta HSTN takes the Ray-Ban Meta idea and shifts the optimization target from ordinary fashion eyewear toward active use. It combines a 12 MP camera, open-ear audio, Meta AI, 3K capture, IPX4 water resistance and up to eight hours of typical battery life in an Oakley frame, with PRIZM lens options on some models. The result is not merely a rebadge: its styling, durability assumptions and audience make it a better fit for people who want camera-and-AI glasses during outdoor activity than for someone prioritizing discretion in an office. Its central tradeoff remains familiar across Meta's line—**excellent integration and mature AI functionality, but comparatively little freedom to replace the software stack or intelligence provider**.

Source: [Meta — Introducing Oakley Meta HSTN](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/06/introducing-oakley-meta-glasses-a-new-category-of-performance-ai-glasses/)

## GLS-0008 — Oakley Meta Vanguard

Oakley Meta Vanguard pushes the performance concept much farther than HSTN. It is a wraparound sports device with IP67 protection, a centered 12 MP wide-angle camera capable of 3K capture, stronger open-ear speakers, a wind-optimized five-microphone array and up to nine hours of battery life. More interestingly, it connects the glasses to Garmin, Strava and health-data ecosystems so Meta AI can surface training metrics and even trigger activity-aware capture. That makes Vanguard one of the clearest examples of smart glasses becoming **situational equipment rather than generic eyewear**: the frame, audio, camera, sensors and integrations are designed around running, cycling and training. It gives up some normal-eyeglasses discretion in exchange for a much more purposeful athletic computer.

Source: [Meta — Oakley Meta Vanguard](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/09/oakley-meta-vanguard-performance-ai-glasses-sports/)

## GLS-0013 — Bose Frames Alto

Bose Frames Alto belongs to the first serious wave of audio glasses: no camera and no display, just ordinary-looking eyewear used as an open-ear listening surface. Alto combined Bluetooth audio, a microphone and a 9-axis head-motion sensor in a roughly 45 g frame, with about 3.5 hours of music playback. The unusual historical piece is Bose AR, which tried to create an **audio augmented-reality layer** by combining head motion from the glasses with location and orientation from a phone instead of drawing graphics in front of the eye. Bose AR did not become a durable platform, but Alto remains a useful reminder that augmentation does not require a screen—and that software-platform longevity matters just as much as clever hardware.

Sources: [Bose — original Frames announcement](https://www.bose.com/pressroom/bose-announces-frames-a-revolutionary), [GlassesResearch — Bose Frames family](BoseFrames/README.md)

## GLS-0014 — Bose Frames Rondo

Rondo is essentially the round-frame sibling of Alto and therefore shares the same underlying argument: smart eyewear can add computing without adding a visible display. Bluetooth audio, a microphone and head-motion sensing turned the frame into a discreet headphone and, during the Bose AR experiment, a spatial-audio interface. Its approximately 3.5-hour playback life looks modest beside newer audio glasses, but Rondo's importance is less about endurance than **social form factor**—it was electronics packaged as recognizable fashion eyewear at a time when many wearables still looked overtly technological. Like Alto, its legacy also illustrates the danger of depending on a proprietary experimental platform that can disappear while the physical glasses remain.

Sources: [Bose — original Frames announcement](https://www.bose.com/pressroom/bose-announces-frames-a-revolutionary), [GlassesResearch — Bose Frames family](BoseFrames/README.md)

## GLS-0015 — Bose Frames Tempo

Tempo is the most purpose-built member of the Bose Frames family. Rather than hiding audio electronics in fashion sunglasses, Bose optimized the frame for running, cycling and climbing: TR90 construction, secure nose and temple contact, IPX4 resistance, helmet compatibility, two 22 mm drivers, beam-forming microphones, USB-C charging and up to eight hours of battery life. The result is closer to **sports equipment with an open-ear audio system** than to general smart glasses. That specialization is its strength—environmental awareness and audio can coexist during activity—but it also defines the ceiling: there is no camera, HUD or active developer platform, so Tempo is best evaluated as exceptionally integrated wearable audio rather than as an extensible computer.

Source: [Bose — Frames Tempo specifications](https://global.bose.com/content/consumer_electronics/b2c/north_america/websites/en_ca/product/bose_frames_tempo.html)

## GLS-0016 — Bose Frames Tenor

Tenor took the second-generation Bose electronics in the opposite direction from Tempo: back toward normal everyday sunglasses. Bose gave it improved open-ear acoustics, 16 mm speakers, dual beam-forming microphones, touch volume control and roughly 5.5 hours of battery life while keeping the frame comparatively conventional. It is a good example of **wearability winning by subtraction**—there is no camera to explain, no HUD in the sightline and no complex interaction model; the glasses primarily make calls, play audio and reach the paired phone's voice assistant. That simplicity also means limited owner programmability. Tenor succeeds or fails mostly as eyewear plus headphones, not as a platform waiting to be repurposed.

Source: [Bose — 2020 Frames announcement](https://www.bose.com/pressroom/new-bose-frames)

## GLS-0017 — Bose Frames Soprano

Soprano shares Tenor's second-generation audio architecture but packages it in a more overtly fashion-oriented cat-eye frame. It has the same basic virtues—open-ear listening, improved bass over the first generation, dual beam-forming microphones, touch controls and about 5.5 hours of playback—without pretending to be a visual-computing device. What makes Soprano useful to the history of smart glasses is precisely that **style was treated as a core technical requirement** rather than decoration around the electronics. The tradeoff is the same as Tenor's: a polished consumer accessory with little evidence of an enduring developer ecosystem, but a strong demonstration that wearable technology has to survive the fashion test if people are expected to put it on their face.

Source: [Bose — 2020 Frames announcement](https://www.bose.com/pressroom/new-bose-frames)

## GLS-0018 — Razer Anzu

Razer Anzu approached audio glasses from a PC-and-gaming brand's perspective. The 2021 frame combined two 16 mm open-ear drivers, microphones, touch controls and low-latency Bluetooth with interchangeable blue-light-filtering and polarized sunglass lenses; Razer also explicitly supported prescription-lens replacement through a partner. At roughly 43–48 g and around five hours of battery life, Anzu was less an attempt at augmented reality than a way to make **headphones disappear into eyewear used at a desk or on the move**. Its gaming mode and lens package gave it a distinct identity, but there is no display, camera or meaningful open-development story, so its intelligence remains mostly whatever the paired phone or computer can provide.

Sources: [Razer — Anzu launch](https://www.razer.com/newsroom/product-news/razer-anzu-smart-glasses-combine-multi-lens-eyecare-and-open-ear-audio-to-connect-in-style), [Razer — Anzu support](https://mysupport.razer.com/app/answers/detail/a_id/4143)

## GLS-0020 — HUAWEI × GENTLE MONSTER Eyewear II

Huawei's second Gentle Monster collaboration is a particularly clear example of smart glasses being designed as **fashion audio wearables rather than miniature headsets**. The electronics provide open-ear sound, dual microphones, Bluetooth 5.2 and touch interaction inside frames weighing roughly 44–45 g, with up to five hours of music playback and IP54 protection. There is no camera or display; the value proposition is that calls, music and phone-assistant access can live in eyewear without advertising themselves as a computer. The collaboration also foreshadowed Huawei's later Eyewear line, where battery life and frame variety improved substantially. Its limitation is equally clear: it is polished connected eyewear, but not an owner-programmable visual-computing platform.

Source: [Huawei — Eyewear II specifications](https://consumer.huawei.com/jp/wearables/gentle-monster-eyewear2/specs/)

## GLS-0021 — HUAWEI Eyewear 2

HUAWEI Eyewear 2 shows how mature display-less smart glasses can become when the engineering focus shifts from novelty to **all-day ordinary use**. Depending on frame, Huawei lists weights of roughly 36.6–43 g including lenses, Bluetooth 5.3, wear detection, motion and capacitance sensors, dual-device connections, IP54 protection and up to 11 hours of listening or nine hours of calls. The frame supports touch gestures and fast charging while remaining compatible with Android, iOS and Windows through standard Bluetooth, although Huawei devices get tighter quick-pair integration. There is still no camera or HUD, but that is not a missing feature so much as the product's thesis: make useful connected eyewear that behaves more like glasses and headphones than like a face-mounted computer.

Sources: [Huawei — Eyewear 2 specifications](https://consumer.huawei.com/en/audio/huawei-eyewear-2/specs/), [Huawei — Eyewear 2](https://consumer.huawei.com/en/audio/huawei-eyewear-2/)

## GLS-0026 — Solos AirGo 2

AirGo 2 belongs to the audio-first branch of Solos' ecosystem: lightweight connected eyewear built around microphones, open-ear audio, sensors and phone-side intelligence rather than a display or outward-facing camera. What makes the family more interesting than a generic Bluetooth headset is the platform continuity around it—Solos later exposed BLE control, sensor access and mobile SDK pathways across newer AirGo generations. AirGo 2 therefore matters as an early step toward **consumer glasses that can become programmable interfaces rather than sealed accessories**. Its limitation is generational: it predates the richer camera and streaming capabilities of later AirGo V models, so it is best understood as the foundation of the ecosystem rather than its technical peak.

Sources: [Solos product site](https://solosglasses.com/), [GlassesResearch — Solos AirGo family](SolosAirGo/README.md)

## GLS-0027 — Solos AirGo 3

AirGo 3 is one of the more compelling display-less smart-glasses designs because Solos treats audio eyewear as a software platform instead of merely a pair of Bluetooth speakers. The current developer program covers AirGo 3 with BLE control, microphone and audio I/O, motion and compass sensors, touch gestures and voice-activation detection through mobile SDKs. There is no camera or HUD, but the absence of those components keeps the product focused on **voice, hearing and sensor-driven interaction while preserving ordinary-eyewear form**. For owner-control research, the important distinction is that developers have documented interfaces to work with rather than being forced to reverse-engineer a closed companion app from scratch.

Source: [Solos SDK / Developer Program](https://solosglasses.com/pages/developers)

## GLS-0028 — Solos AirGo Vision

AirGo Vision is where Solos' audio-glasses lineage crosses into visual AI. By adding a camera to a platform that already exposes audio, microphones, sensors and touch, the glasses can act as a wearable perception endpoint while the paired phone or external services provide the heavier intelligence. Solos' developer documentation exposes camera/photo access on supported AirGo V hardware, which makes Vision more interesting to us than a camera whose output is trapped inside one vendor application. The architecture is still phone- and service-assisted rather than a self-contained local-AI computer, but that can be a strength: **the glasses become sensors and interfaces while the owner has more freedom over where reasoning happens**.

Source: [Solos SDK / Developer Program](https://solosglasses.com/pages/developers)

## GLS-0029 — Solos AirGo V2

AirGo V2 pushes Solos' developer-friendly camera architecture further by adding Wi-Fi data transport, video streaming and recording, webhooks and RTMP endpoints to the BLE/audio/sensor foundation. That combination makes it unusually useful for experimentation because the camera is not just there to feed a single branded assistant; developers can route media and events into their own workflows. In practical terms, V2 is less about putting a giant computer in the frame and more about building a **networked wearable sensor node with open integration points**. The tradeoff is that richer streaming naturally increases network and battery dependence, but from an owner-control perspective the ability to choose endpoints is far more valuable than a closed cloud-only pipeline.

Source: [Solos SDK / Developer Program](https://solosglasses.com/pages/developers)

## GLS-0039 — W610 / HeyCyan ecosystem

W610 is the model that turned GlassesResearch from cataloging into hands-on investigation. Our unit identifies over Bluetooth as `HeyCyan Glasses`, concentrates most electronics in the right temple, uses two right-side controls and a hinge-area status LED, and belongs to a broader OEM/rebrand ecosystem rather than a single clean retail identity. The compelling question is not whether its stock assistant is impressive; it is whether inexpensive commodity camera/audio glasses can be **reclaimed as an owner-controlled wearable interface**. That is why we have deliberately prioritized BLE behavior, firmware, lineage and community tooling over simply installing the vendor app. W610's report card remains incomplete because direct evidence matters more here than filling blanks with assumptions.

Sources: [GlassesResearch — W610 chapter](W610/README.md), [HeyCyan lineage](../lineages/HEYCYAN.md)

## GLS-0048 — Even Realities G2

Even Realities G2 is one of the clearest answers to the question, “Can smart glasses still just look like glasses?” At about 36 g, it packages binocular 640×350 green MicroLED waveguides, four microphones, BLE, IP65 protection and prescription support into a frame designed around discretion rather than camera capture. There is no outward-facing camera and no speaker, so G2's job is **quiet visual information rather than recording the world**. That gives it exceptional wearability and a focused HUD experience, but the elegance comes with a control tradeoff: many functions rely on the Even companion app, internet connectivity and cloud-assisted services rather than an open firmware or broad developer stack.

Sources: [Even G2 specifications](https://support.evenrealities.com/hc/en-us/articles/13499229138959-Specs), [GlassesResearch — Even G2](EvenG2/README.md)

## GLS-0051 — Brilliant Labs Frame

Brilliant Labs Frame is one of the rare smart-glasses products where curiosity does not immediately run into a locked door. The glasses combine a 640×400 color OLED, 720p camera, microphone, sensors, Bluetooth MCU and FPGA with public schematics, block diagrams, a documented BLE protocol, Python/Flutter/Lua tooling, public firmware source and even a documented SWD debug path. That makes Frame less polished as invisible consumer eyewear than some closed competitors, but dramatically more valuable as **a computer the owner is actually invited to understand and modify**. Its modest battery and relatively conspicuous electronics are real compromises; its openness is not marketing garnish but a defining hardware feature.

Sources: [Brilliant Labs Frame hardware manual](https://docs.brilliant.xyz/frame/hardware/), [GlassesResearch — Brilliant Frame](BrilliantFrame/README.md)

## GLS-0052 — Brilliant Labs Halo

Halo takes Brilliant Labs' open-development philosophy and aims it at a more complete everyday AI-glasses platform. Brilliant describes a color micro-OLED display, bone-conduction audio, dual microphones, IMU, optical sensing and an Alif Balletto B1 processor with Cortex-M55 CPU and NPU, all on a ZephyrOS/Lua software foundation with roughly 14 hours of advertised battery life. The important part is not just the component list: Halo is being positioned as **open hardware and software with enough local compute to make on-device AI a first-class possibility**. Because it remains a newer platform, shipping maturity and real-world software quality still need evidence, but architecturally it represents exactly the direction GlassesResearch cares about—capable eyewear without assuming the vendor must own every layer.

Sources: [Brilliant Labs](https://brilliant.xyz/), [GlassesResearch — Brilliant Halo](BrilliantHalo/README.md)

## Coverage rule

The target is one useful profile for every canonical GLS identifier. When the evidence is too thin to write one responsibly, the next action is investigation: verify the model, improve or correct the underlying record, and only then publish the paragraph.

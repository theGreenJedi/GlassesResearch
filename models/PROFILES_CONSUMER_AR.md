# Consumer Audio and Emerging AR Profiles

These profiles are the human-readable output of High-Throughput Report Card Batch 02. They explain what the scores mean instead of reproducing the tables.

## GLS-0030 — Lucyd Lyte (1.0)

Lucyd Lyte’s importance is not that it tried to turn glasses into a miniature computer; it deliberately did something simpler and more wearable. The 2021 first generation combined prescription-ready eyewear with open-ear Bluetooth audio, a microphone and access to whatever voice assistant already lived on the wearer’s phone. That architecture dates it technically, but it also gives Lyte an enduring advantage in owner freedom and cloud independence: calls and audio do not depend on a Lucyd server, and the glasses are useful even if the branded software disappears. Lucyd’s obsession with ordinary-eyewear mass, lens choice and optical-store compatibility is why its Wearability score is far stronger than its raw Hardware score. It is best understood as a good pair of glasses that quietly absorbed a Bluetooth headset, not as an AR platform.

## GLS-0031 — Lucyd Lyte 2.0

Lyte 2.0 sharpened the original idea rather than abandoning it. Four open-ear speakers, dual noise-cancelling microphones, Bluetooth 5.2 and roughly 12 hours of playback/call time made the 2023 generation a materially better audio wearable, while Lucyd continued to offer an unusually broad mix of frame styles, prescriptions and lens options. The result is a device whose report card looks almost inverted compared with cloud-first AI glasses: Visual AI and HUD are N/A, Openness and Hackability remain modest because Lucyd does not expose a deep developer platform, yet Wearability and Cloud Independence are excellent. The Lucyd app can add AI access, but the glasses do not become useless without that service. For someone who values ordinary eyewear first and smart functions second, that architectural restraint is the product’s strongest feature.

## GLS-0035 — Vue Lite

Vue Lite belongs to the same “glasses first” school as Lucyd, but its historical documentation is now thinner. What remains clear is the design philosophy: prescription-capable everyday frames with Bluetooth audio and assistant access rather than a camera, HUD or self-contained computing stack. That produces a respectable wearability/cloud-independence profile and very little in the way of visual AI, openness or deep hackability. The missing detail is itself historically useful: current Vue material has moved on to Lite 2, so this generation is exactly the kind of legacy product for which GlassesResearch now uses Wayback recovery instead of inventing specifications from newer descendants. Until that archival pass is complete, Vue Lite should be read as a well-established audio-eyewear generation with deliberately bounded claims.

## GLS-0036 — Vue Pro (Original Vue)

Vue’s own current accessory and warranty pages still identify Vue Pro as the “Original Vue,” making it a useful marker in the company’s lineage even though the original product pages have largely vanished from the live web. It was an early attempt to make connected prescription eyewear look and behave like normal glasses, with a dedicated charging case and companion-software ecosystem rather than an immersive display or camera-first design. Its report card therefore rewards the ordinary-eyewear goal and relative cloud independence while staying conservative on hardware detail, software depth and hackability. More importantly, Vue Pro illustrates why archival research matters: a product can be historically real and commercially important while its technical record quietly evaporates as a manufacturer redesigns its website.

## GLS-0037 — Ampere Dusk

Ampere Dusk is smart eyewear in a wonderfully literal sense: the clever part is the lens itself. Its electrochromic polarized lenses can change tint from a frame control or the companion app, while Bluetooth audio, speakers and microphones add the familiar headset functions. At roughly 26 grams, Dusk is among the most conventionally wearable devices in the catalog, and its physical tint control means its defining feature does not disappear if an app or cloud service does. That is why its Wearability and Cloud Independence scores are much stronger than its Software, Openness or Hackability scores. Dusk does not understand what you are looking at and it does not project information into your view; instead, it demonstrates that “smart glasses” can improve the optical behavior of glasses themselves without turning them into a face computer.

## GLS-0057 — INMO Air

The original INMO Air matters because it arrived early in a category that is still difficult today: self-contained wireless AR glasses. INMO’s own history records commercial delivery in 2022 and positions Air as an early mass-produced all-in-one wireless AR product. That ambition gives it more architectural independence than phone-peripheral displays, but surviving first-party material no longer exposes enough generation-specific detail to pretend we know every optical, AI or developer characteristic. Its card therefore contains explicit unknowns rather than inherited Air 2 specifications. Historically, Air is the point where INMO’s thesis becomes clear: put display, compute and battery on the head, accept some wearability compromise, and remove the cable and external compute puck. Wayback recovery should eventually let us fill in the missing engineering detail without rewriting history from memory.

## GLS-0058 — INMO Air 2

Air 2 turns that first-generation thesis into a much clearer product. INMO documents dual full-color Micro-OLED waveguide displays, a 26-degree binocular field of view, onboard quad-core compute, IMOS 2.0, Bluetooth, two forward cameras and lightweight SLAM. In other words, this is not merely a notification display hanging off a phone; it is a self-contained AR computer capable of seeing, positioning and running its own software. The tradeoff appears plainly in the report card: Hardware and Display/HUD are strong, Visual AI is real, and Cloud Independence benefits from local compute, but Wearability cannot match ordinary-looking audio glasses or lightweight HUD peripherals. Air 2 is a useful dividing line between “smart eyewear” and “wearable computer”: more capable, more autonomous, and inevitably more demanding on the face.

## GLS-0059 — INMO GO

INMO GO takes a very different route from Air. Instead of maximizing standalone AR capability, it focuses the display on a highly legible everyday problem: real-time translation and transcription. That narrower purpose improves social wearability and makes the HUD genuinely useful without requiring a wide immersive field of view. It also changes the ownership story. The hardware can function as a lightweight wearable interface, but translation is software/service-heavy and the original GO does not expose the kind of open development surface that would earn strong Openness or Hackability scores. GO is therefore interesting not because it wins the specification race, but because it treats the display as a practical subtitle layer for ordinary life—one of the clearest examples of a HUD earning its place by doing one thing people can immediately understand.

## GLS-0060 — INMO Air 3

INMO Air 3 is where the Air lineage becomes a genuinely formidable standalone platform. The current system combines a 1920×1080 binocular Sony Micro-OLED waveguide display, 36-degree field of view, up to 120 Hz refresh, Snapdragon-class eight-core compute, 8 GB RAM, 128 GB storage, a 16 MP ultra-wide camera, microphones, speakers and onboard sensors. More important for GlassesResearch, INMO also documents developer mode, SDK/API access and broad Android application compatibility. That combination drives strong Hardware, Display, Visual AI, Owner Control and Hackability scores without pretending it is open hardware in the Brilliant Labs sense. The cost is wearability and endurance: packing a real Android-class computer, camera and full-color display into eyewear is still harder to live with than a 30-gram audio frame. Air 3 is compelling precisely because the report card shows both sides—an unusually complete face computer whose remaining compromises are mostly the physics of making that much computer wearable.

# Smart-Glasses Model Profiles — Legacy Display and Early Platforms

These profiles continue the human-readable GlassesResearch catalog. The purpose is not to repeat specification tables, but to explain what each device represented, why it mattered, and the tradeoff that defined it.

## GLS-0040 — Snap Spectacles (1st Gen)

Snap's first Spectacles were deliberately narrow in ambition: a pair of sunglasses with a camera built primarily for short, first-person video capture. There was no display, no open developer platform, and very little attempt to make the glasses behave like a general-purpose computer. That simplicity is exactly why they mattered. Spectacles showed that people might accept a camera on the face if the product looked playful, was easy to understand, and did one thing well. Their limitation was equally clear: **they were capture eyewear, not augmented-reality eyewear**, and nearly all of the intelligence lived in the phone and Snapchat ecosystem.

Source: [Snap — Spectacles](https://www.spectacles.com/)

## GLS-0041 — Snap Spectacles 2

Spectacles 2 refined the original camera-glasses idea rather than abandoning it. Snap improved durability, capture quality, transfer speed and everyday usability while keeping the basic screen-free form factor intact. That makes the second generation historically useful because it represents the moment camera glasses started becoming a repeatable consumer product rather than a novelty vending-machine experiment. The tradeoff remained the same: **excellent social simplicity and increasingly polished capture, but almost no owner programmability or standalone intelligence**.

Source: [Snap — Spectacles](https://www.spectacles.com/)

## GLS-0042 — Snap Spectacles 3

Spectacles 3 introduced stereo cameras, which changed the product from simple first-person capture into something closer to computational spatial media. Two cameras enabled depth-aware effects and more sophisticated 3D processing inside Snapchat, foreshadowing Snap's later shift toward true AR hardware. The frame itself was more conspicuous and fashion-forward than earlier generations, which made it less universal as ordinary eyewear but more ambitious as a technology object. Its key significance is architectural: **Snap was beginning to use the glasses not merely to record what the wearer saw, but to understand scene geometry well enough to modify it**.

Source: [Snap — Spectacles](https://www.spectacles.com/)

## GLS-0046 — North Focals 1.0

North Focals were one of the strongest early attempts to make a display disappear into genuinely ordinary-looking prescription-capable glasses. Rather than building a headset, North used a discreet retinal-style display and a finger-worn Loop controller to surface notifications, navigation, calendar information and Alexa without requiring constant phone handling. The product was expensive, required custom fitting, and ultimately disappeared after Google acquired North, but its design thesis remains important: **the winning smart-glasses interface may be less about maximum graphics and more about putting a tiny amount of useful information in the right place at the right time**.

Sources: [North support archive via Google](https://support.google.com/), [GlassesResearch display FAQ](../docs/faq/05-displays.md)

## GLS-0047 — Even Realities G1

Even Realities G1 revived the discreet-display idea with a much more modern emphasis on weight, aesthetics and prescription-friendly everyday wear. The glasses use a minimalist green heads-up display for navigation, translation, notes, teleprompter-style prompts and AI-assisted information rather than full-color spatial graphics. That restraint is the point: G1 is designed to behave like glasses first and a computer second. The resulting tradeoff is attractive but real—**excellent discretion and low visual intrusion, but a tightly managed companion-app experience with far less owner control than open developer platforms**.

Source: [Even Realities](https://www.evenrealities.com/)

## GLS-0049 — Halliday Glasses

Halliday takes a different route to discreet visual information by placing its display optics in the frame rather than directly in front of the eye. The resulting design aims to keep the lenses looking conventional while still providing a private visual surface for translation, navigation, notes and AI assistance. That makes Halliday interesting as an optical-engineering answer to the same problem tackled by Focals and Even: how do you add a HUD without making the wearer look like they have a headset strapped on? Its central tradeoff is that **clever optics and discretion do not automatically imply openness**; the product remains primarily a vendor-designed experience rather than a general-purpose owner-programmable platform.

Source: [Halliday](https://hallidayglobal.com/)

## GLS-0053 — Vuzix Blade

The original Vuzix Blade sits at the boundary between enterprise smart glasses and a consumer-shaped form factor. It combined a monocular see-through display, camera, Android-based software and standalone application capability in something that resembled heavy sunglasses rather than an industrial head-mounted display. The Blade mattered because developers could treat it as an actual wearable computer, not merely a Bluetooth peripheral. Its weakness was physical: **the software architecture offered much more agency than most consumer glasses, but the size, weight and conspicuous optics made the technology difficult to forget once it was on your face**.

Source: [Vuzix — company history](https://www.vuzix.com/pages/history)

## GLS-0054 — Vuzix Blade Upgraded

Blade Upgraded was an incremental but important refinement of the original Blade rather than a wholly new platform. Vuzix improved memory, performance and practical enterprise usability while preserving the same fundamental standalone Android/HUD architecture. That continuity makes it valuable for understanding Vuzix's product philosophy: unlike many consumer smart glasses that outsource almost everything to a phone or cloud service, Blade remained a self-contained application platform. The tradeoff remained equally persistent—**greater developer and enterprise control at the cost of the lightweight, socially invisible form factor that mainstream eyewear demands**.

Source: [Vuzix — company history](https://www.vuzix.com/pages/history)

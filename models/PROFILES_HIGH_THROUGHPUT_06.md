# Evidence-Derived Profiles — High-Throughput Batch 06

These paragraphs are derived from the evidence package in `docs/report-cards/HIGH_THROUGHPUT_BATCH_06.md`. They are intended as concise public-facing summaries rather than score explanations.

## GLS-0106 — Epson Moverio BT-100

Epson's Moverio BT-100 is one of the earliest commercially sold Android-based see-through wearable displays. It paired binocular micro-projection eyewear with a separate Android controller, Wi-Fi, removable storage, audio and roughly six hours of battery life. Its importance is historical: it demonstrated a purchasable, app-capable see-through display years before modern smart-glasses ecosystems matured. By current standards the two-piece design is bulky, the software environment is dated and the device offers little of today's owner-level openness, but it remains an important early branch in the consumer AR lineage.

## GLS-0107 — Epson Moverio BT-200

Moverio BT-200 pushed Epson's platform from wearable media viewer toward practical smart glasses. The binocular 960×540 display and 23° field of view were joined by a camera, microphone, GPS, motion sensors, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi and an Android controller, while Epson explicitly offered a developer-oriented version. It is still visibly and physically a first-generation AR system rather than ordinary eyewear, but its sensor package and development pathway made it substantially more programmable than many contemporary consumer wearables.

## GLS-0108 — Epson Moverio BT-300

BT-300 was a major Moverio refinement: Epson moved to a lighter Si-OLED optical system while retaining Android compute, sensing and a developer-focused application model. It became particularly visible in drone and enterprise workflows, where a transparent display could preserve situational awareness while presenting telemetry or camera feeds. Its architecture is far more developer-friendly than a closed companion-app product, though firmware and hardware internals remain proprietary.

## GLS-0109 — Epson Moverio BT-30C

BT-30C changed the Moverio equation by offloading compute to the device the owner already carried. Instead of functioning as a self-contained Android computer, it behaves primarily as a USB-C tethered binocular display for compatible phones and PCs. That makes it less autonomous but substantially more cloud-independent and host-flexible: the glasses do not dictate the owner's compute stack. With no onboard camera, it is best understood as a programmable wearable display rather than a visual-AI device.

## GLS-0110 — Epson Moverio BT-35E

Moverio BT-35E adapts Epson's binocular display platform for enterprise integration. Its interface-box architecture supports external hosts while the headset adds camera and motion-sensor capability, giving developers access to more than a simple video endpoint. It sacrifices some everyday wearability for deployment flexibility, but the combination of host choice, documented APIs and local operation makes it a comparatively owner-friendly industrial display even though its firmware and hardware remain closed.

## GLS-0111 — Epson Moverio BT-40 / BT-40S

BT-40 modernized Epson's tethered-display approach with a higher-quality binocular display and USB-C host architecture. The BT-40 can operate as a host-driven wearable display, while BT-40S packages it with Epson's dedicated controller. Epson's maintained Basic Function SDK gives applications meaningful control over supported display and sensor functions. The result is a strong enterprise display platform with excellent cloud independence and respectable developer access, but not open hardware.

## GLS-0112 — Epson Moverio BT-45C / BT-45CS

BT-45C is the most capable Moverio model in this catalog segment. Epson combines a binocular Full-HD Si-OLED display and 34° field of view with an 8MP autofocus point-of-view camera, motion sensors, integrated audio, ruggedized mounting options and USB-C support for Android and Windows hosts. Epson also exposes display, camera, sensor and audio functions through its development tools. That makes BT-45C unusually flexible for industrial remote-assistance and workflow applications, while still stopping well short of the open-firmware and open-hardware benchmark set by devices such as Brilliant Labs Frame and Monocle.

## GLS-0113 — Sony SmartEyeglass Developer Edition SED-E1

Sony's 2015 SmartEyeglass Developer Edition was explicitly sold as a development platform rather than a finished mass-market accessory. Its binocular transparent waveguide displayed monochrome green information while a 3MP camera, motion sensors, microphones, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth linked the glasses to an Android phone. Sony provided an SDK and sold the hardware directly to developers in multiple markets. The system was bulky and short-lived, but as an early commercially obtainable smart-glasses development platform it offered substantially more application freedom than most consumer wearables of its era.

## GLS-0114 — Lenovo ThinkReality A3 PC Edition

ThinkReality A3 PC Edition is best understood as a wearable multi-monitor and spatial-computing accessory for enterprise PCs. The 130 g glasses use Qualcomm XR1 hardware, binocular 1080p displays, an 8MP RGB camera and dual fisheye cameras for 6DoF tracking, while USB-C/DisplayPort keeps the host computer central to the experience. That architecture provides strong cloud independence and lets conventional Windows software remain useful without forcing every workflow through a proprietary cloud service. The tradeoff is that the glasses themselves remain a closed enterprise platform rather than owner-modifiable hardware.

## GLS-0115 — Lenovo ThinkReality A3 Industrial Edition

The Industrial Edition uses the same capable A3 hardware but shifts the software emphasis toward frontline AR. Connected to supported Motorola phones and Lenovo's ThinkReality platform, it supports remote assistance, guided workflows, 3D visualization and managed enterprise deployment. Its 8MP scene camera and 6DoF tracking make the device materially more useful for contextual industrial applications than a display-only headset. Lenovo provides development and management tooling, but the stack is still proprietary and enterprise-controlled rather than deeply owner-controlled.

## GLS-0116 — Iristick G2

Iristick G2 takes a phone-centric approach to enterprise smart glasses: the eyewear supplies the camera, HUD and hands-free interface while the user's smartphone supplies much of the compute and connectivity. That keeps weight and heat off the head and avoids locking the glasses to a dedicated compute appliance. A central scene camera, optical-zoom module, adjustable monocular display and remote-support orientation make G2 especially useful for inspection and field collaboration. Its practical host flexibility is stronger than its platform openness; the device layer itself remains proprietary.

## GLS-0117 — Iristick G2 PRO

G2 PRO refines the Iristick concept into 78 g safety-certified glasses with a 16MP central camera, a separate 5MP module offering 6× optical zoom, 1080p video, adjustable monocular LCD HUD, microphones, speaker and touch/voice control. A hot-swappable pocket-unit battery supports long shifts, while Android and iOS phones provide the main application environment. The design is unusually wearable for industrial camera glasses and gives organizations substantial host choice, though low-level device modification is not part of the product proposition.

## GLS-0118 — Iristick H1

Iristick H1 trades eyewear-like discretion for rugged field capability. The 168 g head-mounted system combines dual high-resolution scene cameras, an optical-zoom camera, a bright OLED HUD, quad microphones, swappable power and IP67 protection, and is designed to integrate with helmets and other PPE. It is one of the stronger visual-capture tools in this enterprise group, but its industrial form factor makes it considerably less wearable in ordinary settings. Like the G-series, it benefits from smartphone-hosted compute while remaining proprietary at the device level.

## GLS-0119 — Iristick G3

Iristick G3 is the strongest synthesis of the company's phone-hosted architecture so far. At about 95 g, the safety-certified frame carries a bright 640×400 OLED HUD, a 16MP wide camera and a second 16MP camera with 3× optical zoom, 1080p streaming, beamforming microphones, touch and voice control, and optional prescription inserts. It connects directly to Android or iOS phones over USB-C, using the owner's phone for compute, networking, applications and power. That makes G3 unusually cloud-independent and operationally flexible for an enterprise system, even though its firmware and hardware are not open in the Monocle/Frame sense.
